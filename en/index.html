WHAT WAS MEASURED

A transformer stores repeated processing blocks, layer after layer. Much of what each block learns is structurally similar to what the neighboring block learns — and that repetition takes up space without adding new information.

This research measures how much of that repetition can be removed, component by component, and the exact price of each removal. Every number comes from models trained from scratch and compared against a same-size baseline, trained on the same machine with the same recipe.

The central finding is that the components of a transformer are not equally redundant. Some tolerate aggressive sharing; others lose quality fast. Treating both the same way is what made earlier attempts fail.

The cost of each cut was quantified per parameter removed. That turns compression into a choice — where to save — instead of a blind adjustment applied to the whole model.

TECHNIQUES WERE ISOLATED BEFORE BEING COMBINED

Before stacking anything, each compression technique was trained on its own and measured against the same baseline. This revealed that the combination does not behave like the sum of its parts: a technique that works in isolation can vanish when combined, and the only way to know is to measure both situations.

The most common mistake in compression is to multiply the factors of each technique and announce the product. This project made that mistake, internally reached a number inflated by two orders of magnitude, and corrected it once the techniques were actually measured together.

THE DATA

Not every parameter costs the same to remove

The two main components of a transformer were compressed separately and the cost was normalized by the number of parameters each cut removes. The difference between them is almost ninefold — and that is what makes compression a choice instead of a blind adjustment.

The frontier between compressing and losing quality

Four configurations measured, three seeds each. Compressing more costs more, but not uniformly: between the first two points compression rises by a third and the cost barely moves. The last point exceeds the criterion and was discarded.

The cost doesn't vanish with scale — it plateaus

The same configuration measured at three model sizes. The cost drops and then stalls. The dashed line is the value that had been recorded as a prediction before the third experiment; it did not hold, and the prediction is recorded as a failure.

WHAT THIS WOULD MEAN AT SCALE

These three numbers do not come from any trained model. They are the ratio measured at small scales applied to sizes this research has not reached — the largest model verified so far has fewer than two hundred million parameters.

The ratio may not hold. It has happened before in this project: a trend that looked stable across two scales stopped holding at the third, and the prediction recorded before the experiment failed. That is why the projection appears separately from the results, and without the color used for what was measured.

HOW IT WAS TESTED

  1. Prediction recorded before the experiment. Every scaling test had its expected result and possible outcomes written to a log before any weight was initialized. Two predictions failed and are recorded as failures, with the predicted value next to the measured one.
  2. Matched parameters. Every comparison is against a baseline of the same size, trained on the same recipe, on the same machine. Compression is always measured on the body of the model, with the vocabulary table excluded — it was exactly that table that masked the earlier error.
  3. Three seeds per point. No published number comes from a single run. When a result depends on crossing a threshold, the criterion is the worst seed, not the average.
  4. Audit before running. Every script is reviewed before execution. Four implementation errors were caught this way, and three of them would have produced a false positive — they would have made us believe in something good that did not exist.
  5. Automatic verdicts require significance. After a script read noise as a trend, every verdict started computing the standard error and comparing it with the spread across seeds before concluding anything.
  6. Bit-for-bit reproducible. Fixed seeds, per-epoch checkpoints with atomic writes, and every published number checked against the files on disk.

WHAT WAS RETRACTED

28× compression

The number came from multiplying two factors that do not multiply. A matched-parameter test brought down the entire claim.

Sixty-two experiments proving compression

None of them compressed. The accounting included the vocabulary table, which masked the fact that the body of the model was larger than the baseline. Nine out of nine verified scales were inflated by between 0.3% and 41%.

The cost of sharing vanishes as the model grows

It shrinks and then locks at a positive floor. The earlier reading came from two measurement points; the third point, recorded as a prediction before the experiment, showed the saturation — and the prediction failed.

A general law predicting compression from architecture

Eight hundred and forty-four formulas tested on forty-two points. The best one explained 13% of the variance. The noise-ceiling analysis shows why: at this scale, training randomness dominates the architectural effect, and identical configurations produced results of opposite sign.

WHAT DIDN'T WORK

Removing the smallest weights

Cutting half of the lowest-magnitude weights degraded quality by more than 50%. The hypothesis that the model was undertrained was never confirmed, so the technique is out of the set.

Factoring matrices into lower rank

It works in isolation and vanishes when combined with the rest. It was measured at four scales and did not improve the result at any of them.

Merging weights by averaging

Combining separately trained blocks by averaging their weights failed completely on already-trained models.

Scaling two axes at the same time

The rule used in the old experiments grew two components together and destroyed compression by construction. It was the root cause of the error in the sixty-two experiments.

Measuring capability at this scale

The task benchmark was run and came back null: every model stayed at chance. It isn't a sample-size problem — the model is too small for the metric to work.

Pushing sharing to the extreme

With matched parameters, taking that axis to the limit worsened quality monotonically. The apparent gain was bought parameters, not compression.

HOW THE PROJECT GOT HERE

  • Start

    A request for a chatbot

    The project started as an attempt to build an assistant. The first model had eighty-one thousand parameters and generated incoherent text.

  • Then

    Networks trained from scratch

    Larger models, trained in limited memory, managed to generate coherent text in three languages. From there the question stopped being how to make it work and became how much of it is necessary.

  • Turn

    Fifteen attempts, zero wins

    In the first experiments on real data the compressed model lost by about 10%, without exception. The diagnosis pointed to two errors — one in training and one in architecture — and fixing them reversed the result.

  • Audit

    The reversal was false

    An accounting check months later showed that the comparison had been wrong from the start. The entire body of results was redone with matched parameters, and most of it did not survive.

  • Now

    A smaller, true result

    What remains is a fraction of what was believed, measured at four scales with three seeds per point, and it is the first thing in the project that would survive an independent review.

LIMITS OF WHAT THIS SHOWS

  • Quality is measured by perplexity on text, not by task performance. The task benchmark was run and came back null, with the statistics recorded.
  • The largest model trained so far is small by the field's standards. Nothing here has been verified at industrial scale, and the extrapolation to large models is arithmetic, not evidence.
  • A single pair of corpora, in English, with short context. Generalization to code, other languages or instruction following has not been tested.
  • The numbers hold for a fixed training budget. There is measured evidence that longer training improves the result, and the extent of that is unknown.
  • A single GPU, a single precision type, one-token-at-a-time generation. Other execution regimes were not measured.

STATUS

The research continues. The next steps are to validate the result at a scale larger than a home graphics card can reach, close the task-test gap — which requires a model large enough to rise above chance — and run the compressed model on an ordinary phone.
